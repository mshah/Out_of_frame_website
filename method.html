The method

Reconstructing
with limits.

The archival photograph is evidence. The surrounding world is an interpretation. We make the difference visible.

01 / Two kinds of image

What survives.
What we infer.

Each study begins with an actual historic photograph. A wider panorama is then developed through research into participants, places, material culture and the circumstances of its making.

These artworks are not recovered wider photographs. The surrounding imagery is a contemporary reconstruction. It can illustrate plausible people and actions without claiming that every detail existed at that precise instant.

In The Royal Ring, George V is preserved in the original sepia photograph. Additional royal guests, mahouts and a photographer appear in a reconstructed setting, with their evidentiary status explained in the annotations.

02 / Reading the evidence

Four levels of certainty.

A

Visible source

Details observable in the selected archival photograph itself.

B

Same event

Documents or photographs from the actual expedition or documented historical participants.

C

Context

Contemporary practice, geographic information or comparative historical reference.

D

Interpretation

Artistic choices about location, moment, appearance or action that the evidence cannot establish.

A person can be documented as present on an expedition (B) while their exact placement in our reconstructed scene remains an artistic decision (D).

03 / The viewer’s role

Encounter first.
Evidence second.

The initial visual experience is intentionally open. Visitors need not already understand Nepalese shikar, hunting-ring techniques or the social arrangements of an expedition.

Interactive annotations then offer a second reading: who is credited, who worked, what animals endured, and how photography transformed an elite pursuit into public memory.

The series asks what changes when a photograph of a powerful individual is viewed alongside the larger human and nonhuman operation that sustained the event.
