# The Performance of Power

*Thematic curatorial essay · Working draft v0.1*

A royal hunt could be described as sport. But it was seldom only sport.

When a ruler or celebrated public figure travelled to pursue a dangerous animal, the event carried an audience long before the camera appeared. Invitations had been issued. Permission to enter particular territories had been arranged. Elephants or horses, vehicles, firearms, camps and provisions had been assembled. Guides and attendants had been mobilised. The hunt announced that its central participant possessed access and resources beyond those of ordinary people.

The image of a single hunter facing a formidable animal might suggest an encounter between two individuals. The larger event was often an exercise in organising—and visibly commanding—many others.

## Courage, masculinity and authority

Elite big-game hunting offered an especially vivid way to project courage. Tigers, buffaloes and rhinoceroses could be represented as tests of strength, nerve and determination. The claim to prowess did not always depend on a lone confrontation; it could be expressed through taking part in a complex, managed pursuit. But the final photograph could distil the day into an apparently simple relationship between hunter and quarry.

Such images also helped construct a particular version of masculinity: composed, decisive, physically capable, and entitled to master threatening environments. This was a political image as much as a personal one. The celebrated hunter might be a king, a colonial official, a prince or a former president. His body and his reputation could stand in for an institution, a nation or an idea of leadership.

Yet different hunters made different claims. A British monarch on a diplomatic visit, an Indian ruler hosting his guests and an American naturalist-president travelling with museum collectors did not inhabit interchangeable roles. Treating them all as one kind of imperial hero would flatten the very structures the series hopes to examine.

## The host is part of the spectacle

Consider George V's December 1911 hunt in Nepal. The British king appears as the famous visitor, but the operation depended upon Nepalese political and practical organisation. Chandra Shumsher, Nepal's prime minister and the royal host, had his own standing, resources and diplomatic purposes. The impressive hunting party could display the host's capacity to provide an experience worthy of a king.

The same event could therefore perform power in more than one direction. For the guest, it offered a memorable royal exploit. For the host, it could demonstrate sovereignty, wealth, access to land and the ability to command a sophisticated operation. For other invited participants, it established proximity to authority.

A commemorative image might place the visiting dignitary at its centre while leaving that negotiation almost invisible. Widening the scene does not simply reveal the host's presence; it raises the question of who organised the conditions in which royal prestige could be produced.

## A hierarchy arranged for the camera

Power also determined who could appear in the picture and how. Some participants were expected to pose, others to hold equipment, manage animals or remain available outside the frame. A photograph could make different positions seem natural: the hunter stands beside the trophy, companions face the lens, workers continue their tasks, and the animal becomes evidence of the occasion.

None of this proves that every image was consciously staged as propaganda. Many were souvenirs, personal records or photographs made by people with mixed motives. Their political significance can lie in the conventions they repeat rather than in an explicit programme by their makers.

The camera is useful precisely because it preserves those conventions. The central figure, the arrangement of bodies, the treatment of the animal and the identities supplied by later captions all become evidence of whose achievement was made easy to remember.

## Looking again

*Out of Frame* returns to these scenes not to deny the skill, danger or excitement historically associated with hunting, but to change the scale of the question. What appears to be individual courage also required land, permission, diplomacy, technical knowledge and labour. The apparent solitude of power was often a collective production.

The expanded frame cannot prove every person stood where the artwork places them. It can, however, make the machinery of the spectacle available for scrutiny.

The question becomes less simply **Who killed the animal?** and more **What kind of authority made this image possible, and who benefited from the way it was remembered?**
